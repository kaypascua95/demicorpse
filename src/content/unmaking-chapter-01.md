Anna set two cups on the dining-room table.

She had taken them from the cupboard without looking: the white one with a hairline crack in its glaze, and the heavier blue one with a handle broad enough for her mother's swollen fingers. She placed the blue cup beside the window, where the morning light had always been best.

Only when she reached for the milk did she notice what she had done.

Across the table, her mother's chair was empty.

Evelyn had been dead for twenty-three days.

There had been a funeral, flowers, and nearly two weeks spent sleeping on her sister's sofa. Yet some stubborn part of Anna still expected to hear her mother calling from the sitting room, asking whether the kettle had boiled.

She set the milk down and looked at the blue cup. She had remembered exactly how her mother took her tea, but forgotten that there was no longer anyone to drink it.

It wasn't the first time she had made tea for two.

***

Anna had been ten when her father left.

It happened on a Thursday, after an argument that ended with the front door slamming hard enough to rattle the glass in the hall. By Saturday, several of his shirts were missing from the wardrobe. Her older sister, Ruth, who was fourteen and understood considerably more than Anna did, told her not to ask questions.

"He needs some time," Ruth said.

"How much?"

"I don't know."

"Is he coming back?"

Ruth stopped pulling on her tights. For a moment she looked as though she might cry, but instead she reached beneath the bed for her other shoe.

"Ask Mum."

Anna didn't.

The following morning, she came downstairs to find two cups waiting on the table. Her father's chair had been pushed neatly beneath it, and his shoes were no longer by the back door.

Evelyn sat beside the window, reading the newspaper. Her hair was pinned away from her face, and she wore the pale yellow cardigan Anna liked because its sleeves were soft.

"Morning, darling."

"Where's Ruth?"

"Off to catch her bus. Sit down before your toast gets cold."

Anna examined the cup nearest her.

"Why are there two cups?"

"One for you, silly."

"But I don't drink tea."

"You do now."

Evelyn slid the cup towards her. She had added so much milk that the tea was scarcely brown.

"It's easier to make tea for two," she said.

Then she smiled, and Anna, who hadn't seen her mother smile in days, took a cautious sip.

For a little while, things felt normal. Evelyn read aloud from the newspaper, giving an elaborate voice to a lost dog found miles from home. Anna laughed so hard that tea went up her nose.

But when she asked whether her father was coming back, her mother stopped smiling.

"We'll manage, darling."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know."

Evelyn folded the newspaper.

"Drink your tea before it gets cold."

So Anna drank it.

For years afterward, they began each morning the same way. There were good mornings, when Evelyn made eggs and laughed about the neighbours, and quieter ones when she stayed upstairs until Anna brought her tea.

Anna learned which cup to use, how long to leave the tea, and when her mother wanted company without asking for it. She liked being useful. She liked hearing that she was a good girl.

By fourteen, she knew how to operate the washing machine, which bills needed paying, and where Evelyn kept the spare batteries. By sixteen, she had become accustomed to checking whether her mother was all right before agreeing to go anywhere.

Evelyn rarely told her she couldn't leave. That was what made it difficult to explain.

One Friday, Anna came home with an invitation to spend the night at her friend Claire's house. She had already packed when Evelyn called from the kitchen.

"Will you be home for supper?"

"Actually, I might stay at Claire's."

"Oh."

"Just tonight."

"Of course. Go and enjoy yourself."

Evelyn turned back to the potatoes she was peeling.

"I'll have to remember how much to make for one."

It was a perfectly ordinary remark. Still, Anna pictured her mother sitting alone at the table and felt an uncomfortable tightening in her chest.

She called Claire and said she wasn't feeling well.

Ruth found her watching television later.

"I thought you were going out."

"Changed my mind."

"Was Mum upset?"

"No."

"Then why are you here?"

Anna pulled the blanket over her knees. "Because I want to be."

Ruth sat beside her.

"You know you don't have to sit with her every evening."

"I don't."

"You sort of do."

Anna turned up the television.

Ruth let the matter drop.

At nineteen, Anna was offered a job two hundred miles away. She came home with the letter folded inside her handbag, afraid that if she left it there much longer, she would convince herself not to show anyone.

Evelyn read it twice.

"Two hundred miles," she said.

"It's a good opportunity."

"Of course."

Her mother folded the letter carefully.

"Does Ruth know she'll have to look in on me?"

Anna stared at her.

"She's already here all the time."

"Not all the time."

"Mum."

"I'm only asking."

Anna looked down at the letter.

"You could say congratulations."

Evelyn's expression tightened.

"Well done, then. I didn't say you couldn't go."

"I know."

They didn't discuss the job again that evening.

On the morning Anna left, Evelyn packed sandwiches for the journey, cutting off the crusts as she had done since Anna was five.

"You'll ring when you get there?"

"Of course."

"And on Sunday?"

"Yes, Mum."

Evelyn straightened Anna's collar and embraced her.

"Don't forget to eat properly."

For years afterward, Anna remembered those sandwiches whenever she felt angry about leaving. It was difficult to remain angry with someone who remembered you didn't like crusts.

They spoke every Sunday. Evelyn told her about Ruth's children, the neighbour's extension, and the increasing price of butter. Somewhere during each conversation, she would mention the tea.

"Made two cups again this morning. Out of habit. Silly of me."

At first, Anna told her to stop. Later, she changed the subject. Eventually, she stopped acknowledging the remark altogether.

She had always heard it as an invoice.

But when Evelyn became ill, Anna returned.

Ruth lived ten minutes away and had three children and a husband who worked nights. She could shop, collect prescriptions, and arrange appointments, but she couldn't stay overnight.

Anna could.

She sublet her flat, packed two suitcases, and came home.

"You shouldn't have come all this way," Evelyn said when she arrived.

"It's fine."

"You've got your own life."

"I can manage."

Her mother reached for one of the suitcases. Anna stopped her.

"Don't. I'll take it."

Evelyn withdrew her hand.

"Well, you've nothing keeping you there, have you?"

Anna told herself her mother hadn't meant anything by it. She was frightened and ill. There would be time later to decide how she felt.

But later never came.

Eight months passed between Anna's return and Evelyn's death. Their days were measured in medication, changes of bedding, and calls to the district nurses. Anna learned to sleep lightly and recognise the difference between her mother's requests for water and her attempts to hide discomfort.

There were moments of tenderness she couldn't bear to think about now. There were others she tried not to remember.

Then, one morning, the nurse came, and Evelyn did not wake.

***

Twenty-three days had passed since that morning. Twelve since the funeral.

Anna had spent the intervening nights on Ruth's sofa, where somebody was always losing a sock or asking for a drink. At Ruth's, there was always something to answer. It was easier than silence.

But someone had to meet the estate agent on Friday, and Anna could no longer bear being asked whether she had eaten.

So she had returned to the house that afternoon, bringing an overnight bag and a suitcase, although she intended to stay only four nights.

Now she stood beside the table, looking at the blue cup.

She poured milk into both cups before she realised what she was doing.

Then she put the bottle down hard enough that a bead of milk jumped onto the table.

The kitchen clock ticked above the door. At Ruth's, clocks didn't seem to make any noise. Here, each tick sounded like something small and dry breaking between a pair of teeth.

Anna emptied the blue cup into the sink and washed it carefully. She dried it with a tea towel that still smelled faintly of the lavender soap Evelyn had kept among the linen for years, then returned it to the back of the cupboard.

One cup remained on the table.

In the hall, her suitcase stood beside three cardboard boxes labelled KEEP, CHARITY, and PAPER. Ruth had written the labels the day after the funeral, kneeling on the carpet with the brisk determination she adopted whenever she was afraid she might cry.

Everything in the house had acquired a possible destination except Anna.

She carried her tea upstairs.

Her childhood bedroom looked much as she had left it eight months earlier. A phone charger lay across the pillow, positioned so she could answer without sitting up. Beside it stood a glass of water, an unopened packet of oat biscuits, and the green notebook they had used for Evelyn's care.

Anna opened it.

The first pages were orderly: medication, appointments, and observations recorded in her small, upright handwriting. Later entries were shorter.

*1.30. Water.*

*2.15. Changed bedding.*

*3.40. Settled.*

She remembered writing them, but not always the nights surrounding them. There had been mornings when Evelyn thanked her for something she had no recollection of doing.

Anna closed the notebook.

Her phone rang at seven.

"How is it?" Ruth asked.

"Dusty."

"Just let the man measure things on Friday. You don't have to clear the whole house."

"I know."

"I can come Saturday."

"You said." Anna paused. "Sorry. I know you said."

A child shouted in the background, followed by the sound of a saucepan being dropped into the sink.

"Tell him the pasta isn't poisonous," Ruth called.

"Tell him only the green bits are poisonous," Anna said, loudly enough to be heard.

There was a delighted shriek.

"Very helpful," Ruth said, laughing.

"Put some in a tub for me. I'll eat it cold tomorrow. Evidence for the defence."

"I could bring it over tonight."

Anna looked at her unpacked suitcase. For a moment she wanted her sister there so badly that she had to sit down.

"No. You've only just got your sofa back."

"I don't mind."

"I know. Go and eat while it's hot."

"Anna, you can ask me for things."

"I have. Cold pasta. Keep up."

Ruth laughed again.

"Will you ring if it's horrible?"

Anna looked at the green notebook.

"It's a house."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know."

They were quiet. Ruth had begun leaving spaces in their conversations lately, as though Anna might fill them with something she had previously refused to say.

"I'll call tomorrow," Anna said.

"All right. Love you."

"Love you too."

Afterward, Anna ate two slices of toast standing beside the cooker and went through the ground floor opening windows.

The house smelled of flowers left too long in their water, furniture polish, and the rubber underside of the mattress that had been removed from the sitting room. Four shallow impressions remained in the carpet where the hired bed had stood. Anna had tried brushing them out, but they wouldn't disappear.

In the dining room, a mail-order catalogue lay folded on the windowsill. Evelyn had used it for years to wedge the sash whenever the wind came from the west. During her first week back, Anna had asked why she didn't get someone to repair the catch.

"I don't like strangers in the house," Evelyn had said.

Anna had been home for three days. She remembered wondering whether she qualified.

Now she dropped the catalogue into the kitchen recycling and pushed the window open. Cold evening air moved across her wrists.

Behind her, Evelyn's chair stood slightly away from the table, its front legs on the worn flowers of the rug. That had been her mother's preferred arrangement after the bed arrived: far enough away to rise without help, near enough to pretend she might eat there.

Anna must have moved it while making tea.

She pushed it in and continued clearing the house. She emptied the plastic basin from the downstairs toilet, discarded nearly empty bottles of sanitiser, and sorted the unopened supplement drinks into separate bags.

Vanilla in one. Peach in the other.

Evelyn had hated peach.

Anna almost smiled, then realised she had sorted the bottles without deciding whether she intended to keep them.

She took the green notebook downstairs and stood above the PAPER box. It was only a record of things that had already happened and could no longer be changed.

She dropped it in.

At ten, she checked the front and back doors, placed the keys beside the estate agent's envelope, and went to bed.

Sleep came in fragments. A car passed outside. Somewhere, a pipe knocked as it cooled. She dreamed that somebody was asking for water and woke with one hand already reaching towards the bedside table.

There was no one to answer.

At three, she checked her phone. At four, she put it face down.

She had imagined that after Evelyn died, she would finally sleep. Instead, her body continued waiting for the bell her mother had kept beside the bed.

At some point, she drifted off again.

When she woke, the sky was pale.

Anna came downstairs shortly after seven and noticed the cold before she reached the bottom step.

The dining-room window was open.

Only two inches, but enough to admit a narrow strip of morning air.

She stopped.

She remembered closing it. Rain had started while she was washing her plate, and she had pushed the sash down with both hands.

Now damp grit clung to her fingers when she touched the sill.

Something pale showed beneath the raised window.

The catalogue.

The same catalogue she had thrown into the recycling the night before, its bent corner showing half a woman's face above a price.

Anna went into the kitchen and opened the bin. Newspaper, an empty milk carton, the sleeve from the oat biscuits. She removed each item until she reached the damp bottom.

No catalogue.

Perhaps she'd put it back without remembering. She had been tired, moving things from room to room.

But she remembered the sound it made when it landed in the bin.

She replaced the rubbish and returned to the dining room.

The chair was pulled out.

Its front legs rested on the rug.

She reached to push it in, then stopped.

On the table, beside the window, stood the blue cup.

A spoon lay on the saucer beneath it. There was a shallow brown stain inside, and the handle pointed left.

Her mother had been left-handed.

Anna stared at it. She remembered washing that cup, drying it, and moving the other mugs aside to place it at the back of the cupboard.

In the kitchen, the white cup sat in the sink. The tea towel hung over the oven handle, folded neatly into thirds.

She checked the doors.

Locked.

The keys were where she had left them.

For a moment, she considered calling Ruth, but she could already imagine the questions. Had she been sleeping? Had she been getting up in the night?

During Evelyn's illness, Anna had sometimes performed tasks without retaining any clear memory of them. She would find herself standing beside the bed with a glass of water and remember only that her mother had asked for a drink.

Perhaps she'd made tea in her sleep.

It was possible.

Likely, even.

She lifted the blue cup by its rim and carried it to the sink. The water ran brown, then clear.

Only when she had washed it did she think she should have left it alone.

Her phone lit up.

*You sleep?* Ruth had written.

Anna typed *Fine*, deleted it, and wrote *Some.*

She closed the dining-room window, placed the catalogue on the sideboard, and pushed the chair beneath the table.

Three ordinary things. Three things she could account for.

Then she noticed the notebook.

It lay open on the dining-room table, beside a damp circle where the blue cup had stood.

Anna had put it into the PAPER box the night before. She remembered lowering it between old envelopes and electricity bills. It had been the last thing she did before checking the locks.

She approached the table.

The notebook was open to a clean page.

There was a new entry.

A time, followed by three words, written in her own small, upright handwriting.

*4.10. Settled. Leave light.*
